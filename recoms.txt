Midterm Exam Instructions
Numerical Analysis (SCI19 3111)
Note: Students are not allowed to use phones or tablets during the exam. Calculators are
allowed. In addition, each student may bring one A4 sheet of paper with any notes they wish
to prepare (a cheat sheet).
Note: There is no specific dress code for the exam.
The exam will consist of a multiple-choice section and a written section, with a total of 30
points.
Below is an approximate list of topics that may be covered on the exam. Answers to most
questions can be found in the lecture notes and scans of the lectures (available on e-Learning)
or in the references provided therein.

Theoretical Topics
1. The purpose of numerical analysis. Why an exact solution is not always available or
practical to obtain.
2. Exact solution, closed-form solution, analytical solution, and approximate solution (ap-
proximation). Locality of approximation.
3. Approximation errors: absolute and relative error. Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and
Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for a solution given on a discrete set of points.
4. The differences between the standard form, nested (Horner) form, and centered form of
a polynomial.
5. The connection between repeated applications of Horner’s method for evaluating a poly-
nomial and its derivatives at a given point.
6. The truncation error of a Taylor polynomial in integral and Lagrange forms. Understand-
ing the basic relationship between these two representations.
7. The difference between interpolation and approximation.
8. The main types of interpolation, together with their advantages and limitations.
9. The Vandermonde matrix: where it arises in numerical analysis and how to compute
its determinant. Why is the Vandermonde determinant guaranteed to be nonzero in
interpolation problems?
10. Overdetermined and underdetermined systems arising in polynomial interpolation prob-
lems. The number of free parameters in such systems and the corresponding families of
solutions (basic understanding).
11. The purpose of interpolation. The difference between interpolation and extrapolation.
12. Lagrange basis polynomials. Understanding why these functions form a basis and why
they are independent of the function being interpolated.
13. The concept of divided differences and the Newton interpolation formula. The advantages
of the Newton form compared with the Lagrange form.
14. Computational grids (lattices) and their nodes. Uniform and non-uniform grids. Runge’s
phenomenon. Chebyshev nodes.
15. Endpoint interpolation: B´ezier curves, their role in computer graphics.

Topics for Practical Problems
1. General recommendation: review the basic rules of differentiation, including the chain
rule and product rule, as well as basic integration techniques, including integration by
1
parts.
2. Computing approximation errors using the standard formulas for absolute error, relative
error, MAE, and RMSE.
3. Converting between the standard, nested (Horner), and centered forms of a polynomial.
Estimating the number of arithmetic operations (FLOPs) required to evaluate a polyno-
mial at a given point.
4. Using Horner’s method to evaluate a polynomial at a given point. Polynomial division by
ax − b, where a and b are constants, using Horner’s method (synthetic division). Com-
puting the derivative of a polynomial at a given point using Horner’s method. Applying
Horner’s method to polynomials of arbitrary finite degree n.
5. Approximating a function using a Taylor polynomial.
6. Truncation error for Taylor polynomials in integral and Lagrange forms. Estimating the
maximum truncation error on an interval, including the use of the Sandwich Theorem.
Determining how many terms of a Taylor expansion are required to achieve a prescribed
error tolerance.
7. Taylor and Maclaurin series. Determining whether a Taylor series converges to the func-
tion based on the behavior of the truncation error.
8. Polynomial interpolation. Formulating the interpolation problem as a system of linear
equations, including its matrix form. Computing the Vandermonde determinant.
9. Constructing the Lagrange interpolation polynomial and evaluating it at a given point.
10. Writing the first- and second-order divided differences for a given function (basic under-
standing only; no Newton interpolation formula is required).
11. Constructing simple B´ezier curves from given points.
